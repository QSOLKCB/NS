# Research Charter

QSOL NS exists to investigate the 3D incompressible Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem with unusually strict separation between discovery, reproducibility and proof.

## Principles

1. **Competing hypotheses.** Global regularity and finite-time breakdown remain live until ruled out mathematically.
2. **Reproduce before promote.** Computational signatures must survive independent reruns, resolution changes and precision checks before they are treated as evidence.
3. **State the bridge.** Any inference from discrete computation to continuum mathematics must identify the theorem or argument that justifies the passage.
4. **Preserve failures.** Null results, failed conjectures and failed proof attempts are research assets when they constrain the search space.
5. **Exact provenance.** Evidentiary outputs are tied to source commit, environment, configuration and hashes.
6. **Adversarial mathematics.** Seek counterexamples to every candidate lemma and alternative explanations for every numerical anomaly.
7. **Formal tools have bounded authority.** A proof assistant establishes the encoded proposition relative to its logic and assumptions; it does not establish that the encoding matches nature or the Clay statement without an external correspondence argument.
8. **No prestige language as evidence.** The strength of a result is determined by proof/evidence status, not by the target problem's importance.

## Workstreams

- theorem-boundary and prior-art reconstruction;
- a priori estimates and continuation criteria;
- vortex stretching and concentration diagnostics;
- scale-local energy/enstrophy analysis;
- candidate singularity construction and falsification;
- deterministic numerical experiments;
- interval/high-precision validation where useful;
- machine-checked lemmas where useful;
- publication and archival provenance.

## Success conditions

The project may succeed without solving the Millennium problem if it produces reproducible negative results, rules out candidate mechanisms, sharpens a known bound, formalizes a useful lemma, identifies a numerical pathology, or contributes a genuinely new partial theorem.
